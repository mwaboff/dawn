A countdown tracks progress toward an event, consequence, or adversary move. It starts at a number, ticks down, and when it reaches 0 the thing happens — right after the roll that got it there is resolved.

Three things to decide

Activation
What moment in the fiction starts it? "The PCs sneak into the enemy camp."
Advancement
What makes it tick down? Every action roll, or only certain roll results — that's what the type below sets.
Effect
What happens at 0? An enemy's big move, reinforcements, the bridge finally giving way.

You can tell the players outright ("I'm starting a countdown to when the army attacks") or just imply it ("the bridge is starting to crack"). Showing the number builds tension; hiding it keeps them guessing.

How many segments

2–4
Something that should land soon, regardless of how the players roll.
5+
A powerful move or event that builds across a whole scene.
5–10
Typical for progress and consequence countdowns.
4–12
Typical for a long-term countdown spanning sessions.

Running several at once gets complicated — most encounters use no more than one.

When to tick

Standard
Tick down 1 every time a player makes an action roll.
Progress / Consequence
Tick by the roll's outcome, using the table below.
Long-term
Tick down 1 on a long rest.

Progress countdowns run toward something good, consequence countdowns toward something bad. A looping countdown starts over once its effect fires; a decreasing one loops fewer segments each time and ends for good when it runs out.

Running a chase

Make two countdowns of the same size — a Progress one for your party and a Consequence one for the other side. Start the party's at full, and start the escaping side's lower, by however much of a head start they have:

1 lower
A small lead.
3 lower
A decent lead.
5 lower
A substantial lead.

This works the same whether the party is chasing or being chased. Every action roll advances both countdowns off the table above: successes move the party's, failures move the other side's, and a success with Fear moves both. Whichever reaches 0 first decides how the chase ends. The bigger the starting number, the longer the chase runs.

Decide what one tick means up front — a range band in a market chase, or several miles across open country. For the roll Difficulty, start from the quarry's Difficulty and adjust for the terrain and the fiction.
